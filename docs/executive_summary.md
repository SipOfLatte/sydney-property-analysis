# Sydney Residential Property: Where, What and When to Buy

**Prepared for:** Acquisitions Committee · **Prepared by:** Jack (Data Analytics) · **Date:** October 2026
**Evidence base:** 881,664 Sydney metro sales (NSW Valuer General, 2014–2023); 111,870 repeat sales of the same
dwelling; 7,290 houses with bedroom/bathroom detail (Domain); rental bond data by postcode, 2017–2023 (NSW DCJ).
Growth is after inflation unless stated.

---

## Bottom line

1. **Houses outperformed units by a wide margin, and rental income does not change that.** Houses returned 37%
   after inflation over the decade while units lost 2%. Counting rent, total return was **8.0% a year for houses
   against 4.6% for units**. A house now costs 1.9 times a unit, against 1.4 for most of the period.
2. **The best house markets delivered that growth without extra risk.** Woollahra, Willoughby, Ku-ring-gai and Mosman
   led on growth, had below-average volatility, and *rose* during the 2017–19 downturn while Western Sydney fell
   7–10%.
3. **Expensive suburbs grew faster than affordable ones**, which is the opposite of the common view. This held across
   both houses and units.
4. **Buying new apartments off the plan cost about 9% of value.** Those units resold 9% below what the market
   otherwise supported — a measurable, avoidable loss.
5. **Income is the best it has been all decade.** 2023 rents rose about 25% while prices fell, lifting gross yields
   to 2.6% for houses and 4.1% for units.

## Market context

The Sydney median rose 49% over the decade, but inflation took 29 points of that: **real growth was 17%**. Measuring
the same dwelling sold twice (the industry standard method), houses rose 112% and units 52% in nominal terms, roughly
20 points more than median prices suggest. Medians understate growth because new, cheaper stock keeps entering the
market.

The decade contained two full cycles. Prices fell about 9–10% from the 2017 peak to the 2019 trough, rose 52% through
the COVID boom to late 2021, then fell about 6% during the 2022 rate rises. **Sales volume turned before prices did at
both turning points**, which makes it a useful early signal for timing commitments.

## Where to buy

**Proven performers (buy for compounding growth):**

| Council | Real growth a year | Volatility | 2017–19 downturn |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Woollahra | 7.0% | 6.4% (low) | **+4.0%** |
| Mosman | 6.7% | 7.5% | +1.2% |
| Ku-ring-gai | 6.6% | 7.3% | +2.6% |
| Willoughby | 6.2% | 6.4% (low) | +3.0% |
| *Liverpool (contrast)* | *4.1%* | *8.7%* | *−9.5%* |

At suburb level the leaders were Vaucluse (8.0% a year), Freshwater (7.8%), Bellevue Hill (7.5%), Turramurra (7.4%)
and Avalon Beach (7.3%).

**Value candidates (buy after due diligence):** 30 suburbs combine above-median growth with a 5–22% discount to
comparable areas — including Mount Colah, Frenchs Forest, Hornsby, Thornleigh, Drummoyne, Rose Bay and Randwick.
Three appear on both the growth shortlist and the value screen: **Turramurra, Randwick and Kings Langley**.

**A caution on "cheap" suburbs.** Our value model's cheapest suburbs were Kurnell, Kings Park and Carramar. All three
are cheap for reasons no model can see: a former refinery, an industrial estate, a floodplain. Every discount needs a
local check before it becomes a bid.

## What to buy

Comparing houses sold in the same suburb and quarter, on the same land size:

| Feature | Value added (typical $1.45M house) |
|---|---|
| 3rd bathroom | **+$151k** (+10.4%) |
| 4th bedroom | +$69k (+4.8%) |
| 5th bedroom | +$75k (+5.2%) |
| **6th bedroom** | **nothing (−1.3%)** |
| 1st car space | +$66k · 2nd +$42k · **3rd nothing** |
| +10% land | +$50k (+3.4%) |

Bathrooms carry the biggest premium, though part of that reflects bathroom-rich houses simply being larger and newer.
Treat the figure as a guide to what buyers pay, not as a guaranteed return on a renovation.

## When to buy

Cycle timing matters roughly ten times more than seasonal timing: cycles moved prices 10–52%, while the January
discount is about 3%. January is the cheapest month like-for-like, but listings run about 50% below normal, so there
is less to choose from. March and November are the busiest months and price slightly above average.

---

## Limitations

- **Yields are gross and market-level**, built from median rents and median prices in the same postcode rather than
  matched properties. Costs are assumed at 25% of rent; tax, leverage, vacancy and transaction costs are not modelled.
- **Rent data starts in 2017**, so 2014–2016 is capital growth only.
- Feature values come from a smaller sample (7,290 houses, 2016–2021) and cover houses only.
- Value gaps are measured against fundamentals we can observe (land size, distance to CBD, income, location). They
  cannot see flood or bushfire risk, school catchments, transport plans or zoning changes.
- Data ends in 2023 and excludes the Central Coast and Blue Mountains.
- **Past growth is not a forecast.** The decade studied had falling interest rates for most of its length, which
  favours expensive assets; the "prestige outperforms" finding should be re-tested in the current rate environment.

---

## Proposed next steps

1. **Model net returns properly** for the shortlisted suburbs: management costs, land tax, strata levies, vacancy
   and the fund's financing structure. Gross yields overstate what the fund keeps.
2. **Run due diligence on the three highest-conviction suburbs** (Turramurra, Randwick, Kings Langley): hazard
   overlays, school catchments, transport and zoning.
3. **Extend the data to 2025** to test whether the prestige premium survived the rate-rise period.
4. **Track monthly sales volume** as a leading indicator for the next cycle turn.

## Where the income is

Yields run opposite to prices: in 2023 they ranged from **1.0% in Bellevue Hill** to **6.3% in Lakemba**. Houses in
the premium east and north return 1.8–2.4% gross; the west and south-west 3.4%. Because total return is nearly
identical across those bands, the choice is a **cash-flow decision, not a performance one**.
